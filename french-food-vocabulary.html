French Food Vocabulary: 30 Words with Example Sentences

Bread, cheese, coffee and the bill: the French words you need to shop at a market, order in a café and talk about meals, each shown with its article.

By Melvin, founder of Flashlingo · Published 9 November 2026

Food is one of the easiest ways into French. Menus, market stalls and bakery counters give you constant, low-pressure practice, and many of the words are already half-familiar to English speakers (dessert, café, soupe). The challenge is not the vocabulary itself but the small words around it: every noun has a gender, and French usually needs an article where English needs none.

For that reason every noun below is listed with le (masculine), la (feminine) or l' (before a vowel or silent h, where the gender is not visible, so we add it in brackets). Learn the article together with the noun from day one; it will save you a lot of relearning later. The pronunciation hints are rough English approximations. French stress falls lightly on the last syllable of a word group, so do not punch any syllable too hard.

Usage notes

Du, de la, des: the partitive

When you mean some of something, French uses the partitive article: du pain (some bread), de la soupe (some soup), de l'eau (some water), des pommes (some apples). English often drops the word entirely ("I buy cheese"), but French cannot: j'achète du fromage, never j'achète fromage.

After a negative, it becomes de

In a negative sentence the partitive shrinks to de (or d' before a vowel): je mange de la viande becomes je ne mange pas de viande, and je bois du vin becomes je ne bois pas de vin. This is one of the most common mistakes learners make, so it is worth drilling.

La carte or le menu?

In a French restaurant, la carte is the full list of dishes you choose from. Le menu usually means a set meal at a fixed price, often with a starter, main course and dessert. Asking for la carte, s'il vous plaît gets you the whole menu in the English sense.

Meal names in Quebec

In France, le petit-déjeuner, le déjeuner and le dîner are breakfast, lunch and dinner. In Quebec the names shift: le déjeuner is breakfast, le dîner is lunch and le souper is the evening meal. If you are learning Canadian French, learn that set instead.

Polite ordering

Je voudrais… (I would like) is the safe, polite way to order. Je prends… (I'll take) is also very natural at the table. Avoid je veux… (I want) with waiters; it sounds blunt. Always finish with s'il vous plaît.
