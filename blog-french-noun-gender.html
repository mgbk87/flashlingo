Le or La?

Practical patterns for guessing whether a French noun is masculine or feminine, and the exceptions worth knowing.

By Melvin, founder of Flashlingo · Published 10 October 2026 · Updated 1 November 2026

Why gender matters in French

Every French noun is either masculine or feminine, and that choice affects more than the article. It changes which form of an adjective you use, which pronoun replaces the noun, and sometimes how a past participle is spelled. Say la fromage instead of le fromage and you'll still be understood, but getting gender right makes your French sound noticeably more natural.

For things and ideas, gender is mostly grammatical, not logical. There is nothing especially feminine about a car (la voiture) or masculine about a book (le livre). It is simply part of the word.

The golden rule: learn the article with the noun

The most useful habit is also the simplest: never learn a French noun on its own. Learn la maison ("house"), not just maison. That's why every noun in Flashlingo's French deck includes its article.

One catch: before a vowel or a silent h, both le and la shorten to l', which hides the gender. L'eau ("water") is feminine, but the article doesn't show it. For these words, it's worth noting the gender separately or learning them with an adjective, such as l'eau froide, "cold water", where the feminine adjective gives it away.

Endings that are usually masculine

Word endings are the best clue to gender. They aren't perfect, but they are right often enough to be worth knowing:

  • -age: le fromage (cheese), le voyage (trip). Common exceptions include la plage (beach), la page (page), la cage and l'image, which is feminine.
  • -eau: le bateau (boat), le gâteau (cake). Notable exceptions: l'eau (water) and la peau (skin) are feminine.
  • -ment: le moment, le gouvernement, l'appartement (masculine, though the article hides it).
  • -isme: le tourisme, le réalisme.

Days of the week, months, seasons and languages are also masculine: le lundi, le français.

Endings that are usually feminine

  • -tion and -sion: la nation, la télévision. This is one of the most reliable patterns in French.
  • -té: la liberté, la société. Common exceptions include l'été (summer), which is masculine, and le côté (side).
  • -ure: la voiture (car), la culture.
  • -ette: la baguette, la fourchette (fork).
  • -ence and -ance: la différence, la France. A well-known exception is le silence.

A looser pattern is that many nouns ending in an unstressed -e are feminine. There are so many exceptions, such as le livre, le fromage and le musée, that it is better treated as a hint than a rule.

Words that change meaning with gender

A small group of nouns mean different things depending on gender, and they are worth learning as pairs:

  • le livre (book) and la livre (pound, as a weight or currency)
  • le tour (tour or turn) and la tour (tower)
  • le poste (job or position) and la poste (post office)
  • le mode (method or mode) and la mode (fashion)

People and jobs

For people, gender usually follows the person, and many nouns have a masculine and a feminine form. The endings change in regular ways, which makes them easier to learn as pairs:

  • -eur → -euse: le serveur, la serveuse (waiter, waitress)
  • -teur → -trice: l'acteur, l'actrice (actor, actress)
  • -ier → -ière: le cuisinier, la cuisinière (cook)
  • -ien → -ienne: le musicien, la musicienne (musician)
  • add -e: l'étudiant, l'étudiante (student)

Words already ending in -e often stay the same and only the article changes: le journaliste, la journaliste. For some professions, newer feminine forms such as la professeure are now widely used alongside the traditional le professeur. Flashlingo's deck shows one form per card, usually the masculine, so it is worth learning the feminine form at the same time.

Countries, and when the article disappears

Country names have gender too. Most countries ending in -e are feminine, such as la France, and most others are masculine, such as le Canada or le Japon. There are exceptions, including le Mexique.

Gender also hides in the plural, because les is used for both: les livres, les maisons. And it hides after de in many expressions of quantity, as in beaucoup de fromage. These are more reasons to fix the gender when you first learn the singular, with its article, rather than hoping to work it out later.

Making gender stick

  • Say the article out loud every time you answer a flashcard, so la and le become part of the sound of the word.
  • Notice endings when you meet a new noun, and ask whether it fits a pattern you already know.
  • Collect exceptions in a short personal list. There are fewer of them than it seems, and reviewing them a few times is usually enough.
  • Use Hard mode once a category feels easy. Typing la maison from memory, article included, builds the habit much faster than recognising it in a list.
