Mandarin Tones & Pinyin

How the four tones work, how to read pinyin, and the few tone changes every beginner needs to know.

Why tones matter

Mandarin is a tonal language: the pitch pattern of a syllable is part of the word, not just part of the speaker's mood. In English, saying "yes?" with a rising pitch turns it into a question, but it is still the same word. In Mandarin, changing the pitch on a syllable can turn it into a completely different word.

The classic example uses the syllable ma. Said with a high, level pitch it is mā (妈, as in 妈妈 māma, "mother"). With a dipping pitch it is mǎ (马, "horse"). Same consonant, same vowel, different word. That is why every word in Flashlingo's Mandarin deck shows its pinyin with tone marks, and why it is worth learning the tone together with the word from day one rather than trying to add it later.

The four tones (plus one)

Standard Mandarin has four main tones and a light, unstressed "neutral" tone. Pinyin shows them with marks above the main vowel:

  • First tone (ā) — high and level, held steady like a sung note. Example: 吃 chī, "to eat".
  • Second tone (á) — rising, like the end of a surprised "what?". Example: 国 guó, the second syllable of 中国 Zhōngguó, "China".
  • Third tone (ǎ) — low, dipping. In careful speech it falls then rises; in normal conversation it is often just a low, creaky pitch. Example: 水 shuǐ, "water".
  • Fourth tone (à) — sharp and falling, like a firm "No!". Example: 是 shì, "to be" or "yes".
  • Neutral tone — short and light, with no mark. It usually appears on the second syllable of a word, such as the second ma in 妈妈 māma or the second xie in 谢谢 xièxie, "thank you".

A helpful way to practise is to say a tone in isolation, exaggerating it, then say the whole word at normal speed. Exaggeration feels silly at first, but it trains your ear to notice the difference.

Pinyin letters that surprise English speakers

Pinyin uses the Latin alphabet, which makes Mandarin words easy to type and look up, but several letters do not sound the way an English reader expects:

  • q sounds close to the "ch" in "cheese", with the tongue flat behind the lower teeth.
  • x sounds close to the "sh" in "she", again with a flat tongue.
  • zh, ch, sh are similar to English "j", "ch" and "sh", but with the tongue curled further back.
  • c sounds like the "ts" in "cats".
  • z sounds like the "ds" in "kids".
  • r at the start of a syllable is somewhere between an English "r" and the "s" in "pleasure".

Pinyin is a tool for learning pronunciation, not a replacement for characters. Native speakers read and write in characters, so it is worth noticing the characters even while you rely on pinyin to say the words.

Three tone changes you'll hear all the time

Tones are not completely fixed. A few common rules, called tone sandhi, change a tone depending on what comes next. You do not need to memorise a long list; these three cover most of what a beginner meets:

  • Two third tones in a row: the first one is pronounced as a second (rising) tone. 你好 is written nǐhǎo, but it is said closer to ní hǎo.
  • 不 bù before a fourth tone: it becomes second tone. 不是 ("is not") is pronounced bú shì.
  • 一 yī before other syllables: it usually becomes yì before first, second and third tones and yí before a fourth tone, but stays yī when counting or at the end of a word.

Most dictionaries and word lists, including ours, show the original tone of each character. When you hear a native speaker say something slightly different, these rules are usually why.

How to practise tones with flashcards

Flashcards are a good fit for tones because they force you to recall the whole word, sound included. A few habits help:

  • Say every answer out loud, with the tone, before checking it. Silent review lets you skip the hardest part.
  • Learn words, not single syllables. Two-syllable words such as 中国 or 谢谢 give your ear a pattern to remember, which sticks better than isolated sounds.
  • Group by tone pattern now and then. Noticing that several words share the same first-tone-plus-second-tone shape makes them easier to recall.
  • Keep sessions short and frequent. Ten minutes a day gives your ear more repeated exposure than one long session a week.
