The Science of Flashcards

Why active recall and spaced repetition beat re-reading and highlighting.

The problem with re-reading

Most people study vocabulary the same way they study everything else: they read a word and its translation over and over, hoping it sinks in. It feels productive, because the material starts to feel familiar. But familiarity is a trap — recognizing a word when you see it is a much easier mental task than producing it from memory when you need it, and re-reading trains the easy skill instead of the useful one.

Psychologists call this the difference between recognition and recall. A multiple-choice quiz or a conversation both demand recall: pulling a word out of memory with no prompt in front of you. Passive review never really practices that.

Active recall: making your brain do the work

Active recall means testing yourself instead of reviewing the answer. Cover the translation, try to produce it, then check. This small change — retrieving information rather than re-reading it — has been studied under the name "the testing effect" since at least the 1910s, and it consistently shows the same result: people who quiz themselves remember more, for longer, than people who spend the same amount of time simply re-reading.

The effort of retrieval is the point. Cognitive scientists describe this as a "desirable difficulty" — a small amount of struggle during learning that feels less comfortable in the moment but produces a stronger, more durable memory afterward. A flashcard quiz with a timer, like the ones on Flashlingo, leans into this directly: every question forces retrieval, not recognition.

Spaced repetition: reviewing at the right moment

The second piece is timing. In the 1880s, the German psychologist Hermann Ebbinghaus documented what's now called the forgetting curve: newly learned information fades quickly at first, then more slowly over time. Reviewing a word once isn't enough to fix it in long-term memory — but reviewing it again right around the point you'd otherwise start to forget it resets the curve and makes the next round of forgetting slower still.

This is why cramming a word list the night before a trip feels effective in the moment but rarely lasts — everything was learned in one pass, with no spacing. Spaced repetition systems instead space reviews out over days or weeks, showing you a word again just as it's about to slip, rather than while it's still fresh.

Putting it together in a flashcard quiz

A well-designed flashcard system combines both ideas: it forces active recall on every question, and it resurfaces words on a schedule shaped by how well you already know them — words you get wrong come back sooner, words you consistently get right come back less often. That's a large part of why simple flashcard tools, despite feeling almost too basic to work, regularly outperform more elaborate study methods for the specific job of building vocabulary.

It also explains why short, frequent sessions tend to beat long, infrequent ones. Ten focused minutes a day gives you more spaced retrieval attempts over a month than one exhausting hour every couple of weeks — even though the total study time is similar.
