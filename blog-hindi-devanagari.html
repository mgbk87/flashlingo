Devanagari for Beginners

How the Hindi script works, and the one rule that explains why words are not said exactly as spelled.

By Melvin, founder of Flashlingo · Published 1 November 2026

One script, many languages

Hindi is written in Devanagari, a script also used for Sanskrit, Marathi and Nepali. It is read from left to right, and most letters hang from a horizontal line along the top, so a written word looks like a row of letters pegged to a washing line. Once you know the letters, Devanagari is very regular: words are generally pronounced the way they are written, with one important exception covered below.

Every consonant comes with an "a"

Devanagari is not quite an alphabet. Each consonant letter carries a short "a" sound by default. The letter क on its own is read ka, not just "k". Linguists call this kind of script an abugida.

To use a different vowel, you add a vowel sign, called a matra, to the consonant. The consonant stays the same; only the mark changes:

One sign is written out of order. The short "i" sign ि is placed before the consonant, even though it is pronounced after it. You can see it at the start of किताब (kitāb, book), where कि is read ki.

At the start of a word, vowels use their own full letters instead: अ (a), आ (ā), इ (i), ई (ī), उ (u), ऊ (ū), ए (e), ऐ (ai), ओ (o), औ (au). That is how एक (ek, one) begins with ए.

The silent "a"

Here is the exception. Although every consonant carries an "a", Hindi usually drops it at the end of a word and often in the middle. Linguists call this schwa deletion. Compare the spelling with the pronunciation:

  • घर (ghar, house) is written घ + र, which would be "gha-ra", but it is said ghar.
  • कमरा (kamrā, room) is written क + म + रा, but it is said kam-rā, not "ka-ma-rā".

There are rules for when the "a" disappears, but most learners pick them up by ear. This is one reason Flashlingo shows a romanization with every Hindi word: it tells you how the word is actually said.

When consonants meet

If two consonants come together with no vowel between them, Devanagari usually joins them into a single shape called a conjunct. Often the first letter loses its vertical stroke and leans against the second:

  • विद्यालय (vidyālay, school): द and य join as द्य.
  • धन्यवाद (dhanyavād, thank you): न and य join as न्य.
  • त्योहार (tyohār, festival): त and य join as त्य.

A few conjuncts look quite different from their parts, so it is worth learning them as you meet them rather than all at once.

Dots, moons and other marks

  • Nasal vowels. A dot above the line (ं) or a moon with a dot (ँ) makes the vowel nasal, as in हाँ (hā̃, yes) and नहीं (nahī̃, no). The romanization shows this with a tilde (ã, ĩ).
  • The nukta. A dot under a letter marks sounds that came into Hindi through Persian and Arabic: क़ (q), ख़ (x, like the "ch" in Scottish "loch"), ग़ (ġ), ज़ (z) and फ़ (f). You can see it in क़ानून (qānūn, law).
  • Retroflex sounds. Hindi has two sets of t and d sounds. For dental ones (त, द) the tongue touches the teeth; for retroflex ones (ट, ड) it curls back. The romanization marks retroflex letters with a dot underneath, as in the ṛ of गाड़ी (gāṛī, car).

How to start

  1. Learn the consonants in their traditional order. The chart is arranged by where the sound is made in the mouth, which makes it easier to remember.
  2. Learn the vowel signs on one consonant, as in the table above, before trying them on others.
  3. Read short everyday words: पानी (pānī, water), चाय (cāy, tea), दूध (dūdh, milk), भारत (bhārat, India).
  4. Keep the romanization as a check, not a crutch: read the Devanagari first, then look.
