    📖 About Indonesian

    Why learn Indonesian with Flashlingo?

    Indonesian (Bahasa Indonesia) is the national language of Indonesia, the world's fourth most populous country. Most Indonesians grow up speaking a regional language such as Javanese or Sundanese and use Indonesian for school, work, media and travel — so it's spoken by well over 200 million people in total. It is closely related to Malay, so learning one gives you a big head start on the other.

    How the Indonesian levels work

    The Indonesian deck has three levels of 500 words each, so you can start where you actually are.

    • Beginner — 500 words across 24 topics: everyday essentials such as food, home, transport, numbers and greetings. For example: apel (apple), sepeda (bicycle), telepon (phone).
    • Intermediate — 500 words across 19 topics: vocabulary for work, travel, technology, health, money and the news. For example: paspor (passport), aplikasi (app), kecerdasan buatan (artificial intelligence).
    • Expert — 500 words across 17 topics: formal and academic vocabulary for law, science, philosophy, economics and the arts. For example: yurisprudensi (case law), kehendak bebas (free will), lubang hitam (black hole).

    A lot of formal vocabulary comes from Dutch, English, Sanskrit and Arabic, so many academic words look familiar — demokrasi (democracy), fotosintesis (photosynthesis) and kurikulum (curriculum), for example.

    Tips for learning Indonesian vocabulary faster

    • Indonesian uses the Latin alphabet and is written almost exactly as it is pronounced, with no tones and no accent marks — you can read new words aloud from day one.
    • Verbs never change for tense or person. Time is shown with separate words such as kemarin (yesterday) or besok (tomorrow).
    • Learn to spot prefixes. The me- prefix turns a root into an active verb: membaca (to read) comes from baca and menulis (to write) from tulis.
    • Many Malay and Indonesian words are identical, but everyday words can differ — Indonesian says mobil (car) where Malay says kereta, and kantor (office) where Malay says pejabat.
    • Plurals are usually left to context, or shown by repeating the word (anak-anak, "children").

    A few words to start with

    selamat pagi (hello / good morning), terima kasih (thank you), ya (yes), tidak (no) and sama-sama (you're welcome) will get you through most everyday exchanges.

    FAQ

    Is Indonesian the same as Malay?

    They are two standard forms of the same language family and are largely mutually intelligible, but spelling and many everyday words differ. This deck uses standard Indonesian — try the Malay deck too and compare.

    Why do some verbs start with me- or ber-?

    These prefixes mark active and intransitive verbs. The deck shows the form you would normally hear in a sentence, which is sometimes the bare root.

    Is Indonesian easy for English speakers?

    It's often rated one of the more approachable Asian languages: Latin script, no tones, no verb conjugation and no grammatical gender.
