How Hangul Works

The Korean alphabet was designed to be learned fast. Here is how it fits together.

By Melvin, founder of Flashlingo · Published 1 November 2026

A script designed to be learned quickly

Most writing systems grew slowly over centuries. Hangul, the Korean alphabet, was designed on purpose. It was created in the 1440s under King Sejong of the Joseon dynasty and announced in 1446 in a document called the Hunminjeongeum, "the correct sounds for the instruction of the people". At the time, Korean was written with Chinese characters, which took years to learn, so most people could not read. Hangul was meant to fix that.

It worked. Hangul is often described as one of the most logical writing systems in use, and South Korea still celebrates it every year on Hangul Day, 9 October. For a learner, the practical upshot is simple: you can learn to read Korean (sound it out, not yet understand it) in an afternoon or two.

Fourteen consonants, ten vowels

Hangul has 14 basic consonants and 10 basic vowels. Everything else is built from them: doubled consonants for "tense" sounds and combined vowels for a few extra sounds, which brings the full set to about 40 letters.

Basic vowelsㅏ ㅑ ㅓ ㅕ ㅗ ㅛ ㅜ ㅠ ㅡ ㅣ

The shapes are not random. The basic consonants were drawn to suggest the mouth making the sound: ㄱ (g/k) shows the back of the tongue lifting, ㄴ (n) the tip of the tongue touching behind the teeth, and ㅁ (m) the closed lips. Related sounds add a stroke, so ㄱ becomes ㅋ (k, with a puff of air) and ㄷ becomes ㅌ.

The vowels are built from three elements, traditionally linked to heaven (a short stroke, once a dot), earth (a flat line ㅡ) and people (an upright line ㅣ). Add a short stroke to the right of ㅣ and you get ㅏ (a); to the left, ㅓ (eo). Doubling the stroke adds a "y" sound: ㅏ → ㅑ (ya).

Letters are stacked into syllable blocks

This is the part that looks unfamiliar at first. Korean letters are not written in a long row. They are grouped into square blocks, one per syllable, and each block follows the same recipe:

  • a consonant first, then
  • a vowel, to the right if it is tall (ㅏ ㅓ ㅣ) or underneath if it is flat (ㅗ ㅜ ㅡ),
  • and sometimes a final consonant at the bottom, called batchim.

Every syllable must start with a consonant letter. When it starts with a vowel sound, the silent placeholder ㅇ fills the first slot, so the syllable "a" is written 아. At the bottom of a block, the same ㅇ is pronounced "ng".

Why the same letter can sound different

Notice that ㄱ is written "k" at the end of 학 but "g" at the start of 교. Korean consonants shift slightly depending on their position, and the Revised Romanization used in Flashlingo's Korean deck writes the word the way it sounds, not letter by letter.

Neighbouring sounds also affect each other. In 정류장 (jeongnyujang, bus stop), the ㅇ and ㄹ meet and the ㄹ is pronounced "n", so the romanization shows jeongnyujang even though there is no ㄴ in the spelling. You do not need to learn these rules up front. Read words aloud, check the romanization, and the patterns become familiar.

Korean also has three versions of several consonants: plain, aspirated (with a puff of air) and tense. ㄱ, ㅋ and ㄲ are the classic trio. The difference matters, but it is easier to hear after you have learned to read than before.

Practise on words you already know

The fastest way to build reading confidence is with loanwords from English, because you already know the answer. Try to sound these out before reading the romanization:

  • 커피 (keopi, coffee)
  • 버스 (beoseu, bus)

Korean has no "f" or "z" sound and keeps syllables simple, so English words are adapted, which is why "bus" becomes three letters across two blocks. Once loanwords feel easy, move on to native words such as 어머니 (eomeoni, mother), 나라 (nara, country) and 밥 (bap, rice).

A two-day plan

  1. Day 1, morning: learn the 10 basic vowels. They are drawn from three strokes, so they go quickly.
  2. Day 1, afternoon: learn the 14 basic consonants and how ㅇ works at the start and end of a block.
  3. Day 2: read syllable blocks, then loanwords, then short native words. Say each one out loud.
  4. After that: keep the Hangul in front of you every time you study vocabulary, so reading becomes automatic.
